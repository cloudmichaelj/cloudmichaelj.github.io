Exposition in Applied Mathematics

Some Principles

cloudmichaelj@gmail.com

In my view the term exposition belongs entirely to pure explanation. It is not merely communicating information. It is revealing structure, clarifying ideas, and making difficult concepts accessible without diminishing them. Exposition is both a fine art and a form of intellectual craftsmanship.

Exposition is not just the packaging of mathematics discovered elsewhere; the arrangement of an argument is itself mathematical work. This is especially true in applied mathematics, where argument can move among several kinds of knowledge. Good exposition keeps these movements intelligible.

The following points reflect my decades of writing textbooks and struggling to read bad ones. However, I have tried to set forth principles rather than pet peeves.

Clarity is the main requirement.

Since clarity has its own crystal-like beauty, there is no need for a self-conscious “elegance.” Display of cleverness for its own sake is also unhelpful.

Start with the phenomenon and the question.

Applied mathematics begins with something that needs to be understood. Notation and formalism enter naturally once a question has made them relevant. The reader should know what the mathematics is being asked to do before being required to take up its symbols.

Choices should be made visible.

The phrase “it is convenient to introduce” can hide too much mathematical substance.

Notation should earn its keep.

A heavy arsenal of symbols puts a tax on working memory. It's frustrating to have to figure out which of an author's notational devices to suppress while reading.

Purpose before execution.

Solid exposition makes clear how one mathematical step creates the need for the next. This differs from reproducing the chronology of discovery. A transformation should therefore be understood at the level of purpose before it is executed.

When possible, scale should reveal structure.

Applied mathematics often becomes intelligible when a conservation law is understood as a competition among mechanisms. Scaling makes that competition visible; nondimensionalization exposes the ratios that actually govern the competition.

Approximation as a mathematical statement — not a disclaimer.

Because an exact equality, an asymptotic equivalence, a controlled error estimate, an order-of-magnitude statement, and a numerical observation are not interchangeable, neither are words such as therefore, suggests, approximately, heuristically, and numerically.

The limitations of a model are part of its meaning. No approximation is complete without its regime of validity.

Change viewpoint when structure calls for it.

Algebraic, geometric, analytic, probabilistic, physical, and computational descriptions often reveal different aspects of the same structure.

Structure before computation.

A calculation is not automatically an explanation.

Computation should remain distinguishable from inference. Numerical work is most illuminating when it enters as an instrument of mathematical thought. A graph should exist because there is something mathematical to see.

Teach the reader what to notice.

Many mathematical results contain too much information to be grasped at once. The reader has finite attention and finite capacity to retain provisional information; these are not for the writer to squander. State the hard idea plainly before surrounding it with formalism.

Mature exposition does not compete with mathematics by restating everything in words. The equation contains the structure; good prose directs attention toward structural aspects that deserve notice.

No gratuitous examples.

A worked example should disclose something important.

Counterexamples are quite valuable when they delineate the boundaries of meaning.

Let mathematics change the question.

An argument may show that the original question was badly posed, that another quantity is more natural, that an apparent difficulty belongs to the wrong scale, etc. Good applied exposition does not conceal such realizations after the fact; it permits the mathematics to appear as a means of discovering what the real question was.

But again, discovery should not be confused with chronology. The aim is not to reproduce every false start or past accident, but to preserve the turning points that explain why the successful argument has the shape it does.

Return from mathematics to the phenomenon.

A common failure of technical writing is to end when the calculation ends. Instead, an exposition can return to the original question. The mathematics should ultimately change the reader's picture of the world from which the problem arose.

What can be reconstructed can be left out.

Exposition is an art of omission as much as inclusion.

This is not a call for brevity as an end in itself; terseness can conceal too much, while a long exposition can actually be economical for the reader. The main principle is that once conceptual structure has been absorbed, forgotten details can often be recovered.

The best applied exposition leaves behind a reusable mental object.
