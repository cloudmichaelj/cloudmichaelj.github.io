Mathematical English

Some Observations

cloudmichaelj@gmail.com

Mathematical English (ME) is precise and relatively formal, but it is not just careful academic English with mathematical symbols and vocabulary thrown in. Having spent most of my career writing in this particular English register, I will note what I see as its most diagnostic features. These have a long history in English mathematical writing.

Dynamic Style

The register's general style reflects the need to track often intricate underlying logic. Mathematical prose can show complex layered clause structures and relies heavily on the English connectives (prepositions, conjunctions, relative pronouns, relative adverbs, and conjunctive adverbs) to foreground premises, conclusions, conditions, and exceptions.

We say that f is continuous at a if for every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that |f(x) − f(a)| < ε whenever |x − a| < δ.

This contrasts with the more synoptic style of much scientific and technical prose, one practice of which is to pack information into dense noun phrases:

A condensation reaction of a highly concentrated aqueous solution of ammonia on the simple dialdehyde glyoxal produces glycosine.

Very roughly, scientific English describes the world; mathematical English establishes a framework and then reasons within it.

Grammar Working Together with Mathematical Notation

English grammar carries part of the logical structure in a sentence such as

For every x ∈ X, there exists y ∈ Y such that P(x, y).

Indeed, reversing the order of the quantifiers changes the proposition, and this is reflected in the grammatical order.

The Acquisition of Specialized Meanings

Some ordinary English words (group, mean, prime, etc) acquire specialized meanings in ME. Others (distinct, arbitrary) retain something of their plain meanings while taking on narrower roles.

Tense, Voice, and Person

ME strongly favors the present tense. The passive voice is not quite as central as in scientific English. ME makes extensive use of the first-person plural pronoun we.

High Lexical Density

Lexical density measures the proportion of content words in a clause. A reader must recover relatively large amounts of information from relatively short passages of ME.

Formulaic Phrases

Here I mean phrases such as if and only if, without loss of generality, and such that.

Characteristic Use of the Imperative

ME often employs the imperative mood to guide the reader through a construction or argument: let x be a real number; choose an element of X; fix ε > 0; suppose that f is continuous; consider the following function. These imperatives seldom represent commands in the usual sense.

Imperative let licenses a bare infinitive complement for assigning notation or introducing mathematical objects: let x be a real number; let f denote the function; let P lie on S.

Extended Use of where

In its most familiar use, the relative adverb where has a literal spatial meaning:

Let S be the region where f is defined.

But ME also uses where to introduce a clause specifying a definition, notation, or condition, even when the antecedent is not a place:

Let x = 2a, where a is the radius of the cylinder.

This allows a short relative clause to supplement a mathematical statement without interrupting syntactic flow.

Careful Counterfactuals and Suppositions

Conditionals as plain facts can be stated in the indicative mood.

If p=1, then q = 1/2.

Clearly there is uncertainty as to the fact of whether the condition holds here, but if it does then the consequence is entailed. The statement does not go out of its way to introduce a sense of unreality or doubt, or depict a situation where we are merely imagining something. This is also the case with the following conditional, even though past time is referred to:

If the reader did this, he or she committed an error.

But other types of hypothetical bring unreality to the fore as a way of proceeding with an argument.

When reference is made to present time, the patterns are

If a condition were to hold or could hold, (then) something should or would follow.
Something should or would follow if a condition were to hold.
Were a condition to hold, (then) something would or should follow.
Something would or should follow were a condition to hold.

In this way the past tense is used as the marker of unreality in English:

The case would be altered at once if the ring were not quite uniform, e.g. if it were slightly thicker at one point.

Sir Horace Lamb, The Dynamical Theory of Sound

Again this was for reference to present time. For reference to past time, the past perfect is used; the paradigm is something like

If a condition had held, (then) something should/would have followed.
Something should/would have followed if a condition had held.
Had a condition held, (then) something should/would have followed.
Something should/would have followed had a condition held.

Note the “had / have” pairing.

Of course we never really know what the future holds, but at times we may want to express a supposition and state its consequence as a mere mental construct, or as something doubtful. In this case we use the subjunctive in both clauses of the conditional:

If it occurred / were to occur / should occur, (then) we should / it would ….
If we were to do this, we would find ….

Although the subjunctive mood is often ignored, its use can help writers keep their thinking straight. The same is true for choice of imperative verb. There is no mental conflict in saying “assume x is 1” if 1 is a permissible value of x. But “assume x is infinite” can be problematic if the statement is clearly impossible or we intend to demonstrate its falsehood. In contrast, “suppose x were infinite” frames an imaginative exploration.

For suppose x were infinite. Then … But …, which would be absurd. Consequently … □

The question is whether we can best keep our thoughts straight by “assuming” that something “is” while setting out to show that it actually “isn’t”.

The Present Subjunctive

Beyond the contrast between plain facts and total unreality, ME occasionally employs the present subjunctive to signal a state of "mere possibility" — something imagined or conceived to enable a further statement, without implying that the condition is impossible or unlikely. For verbs other than be, the third-person singular present subjunctive is distinguished from the indicative by the absence of the -s ending. To illustrate the precision this affords, consider the verb lie (past tense lay):

For if P lies on S — present indicative: in some cases P does indeed lie on S.

For if P lie on S — present subjunctive: P is imagined on S; it may or may not be so in reality.

For if P lay on S — past subjunctive: in reality P cannot reside on S.

This classical usage allows precise calibration of the level of doubt or imagination. The present subjunctive also appears in adverbial purpose clauses (e.g., "it is necessary to do so, that the effort might be successful") and in frozen constructs such as "be that as it may."

While the present subjunctive has become less common in contemporary writing, its presence in ME serves to distinguish between implications based on possible conditions and those based on factual conditions.
