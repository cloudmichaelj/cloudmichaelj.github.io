Mathematical English

Some Observations

cloudmichaelj@gmail.com

Mathematical English (ME) is precise and relatively formal, but it is not just careful academic English with mathematical symbols and vocabulary thrown in. Rather, its natural-language expressions interact with symbolic expressions and logical structure in particular ways. Having spent most of my career writing in this particular English register, I will note what I see as its most diagnostic features. These have a long history in English mathematical writing.

Perhaps I should start with a quick discursive profile. In ME, precise conceptual meanings and shared vocabulary are established via explicit definitions:

By an equivalence relation, we mean …

Concepts are organized through explicit classification:

There are precisely four types of …

The progression of mathematical meaning is rendered explicit and traceable via expressions that establish logical relations between statements:

Next we substitute this value into …
Consequently we have …

Justification and the demonstration of validity are provided through detailed argumentation, typically centered on hypothetical and conditional language:

Let n be a positive integer.
If |x| > 0, then x > 0 or -x > 0.

The reasoning is generally presented as systematic, reproducible, and independent of personal opinion:

We now consider the remaining case.
It can be shown that …

Claims are carefully scoped through explicit (or implicit but clear) quantification (“all,” “for every,” “for some”) or limitation (“on a closed interval,” “but not conversely”).

Coherence is maintained through extensive use of reference expressions (“the above result,” “the corresponding solution,” “both solutions,” “these values”).

Dynamic Style

The register's general style reflects the need to track often intricate underlying logic. Mathematical prose can show complex layered clause structures and relies heavily on the English connectives (prepositions, conjunctions, correlative pairs, relative pronouns, relative adverbs, and conjunctive adverbs) to foreground premises, conclusions, conditions, and exceptions.

We say that f is continuous at a if for every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that |f(x) − f(a)| < ε whenever |x − a| < δ.

This very standard sentence is a multiply embedded conditional construction. An outer antecedent (if …) contains an existential clause (there exists …), which contains a such-that clause, which in turn contains a whenever subordinate clause. The consequent (We say that f is continuous) contains a content clause (that f is continuous). ME is replete with such sentences:

Writing α = 2p, we see from (9) that if p > 1/2 then fα is decreasing.
It is clear that if x satisfies (1) then so does nx, where n ∊ ℤ.

Tightly packed chains of conditional statements are used to drive reasoning forward.

We could contrast this with the more synoptic style of much scientific and technical prose, one practice of which is to pack information into heavy noun phrases:

A condensation reaction of a highly concentrated aqueous solution of ammonia on the simple dialdehyde glyoxal produces glycosine.

Very roughly, scientific English describes the world; mathematical English establishes a framework and then reasons within it.

This is not to say that ME is incapable of dense noun phrases:

Consider the set of all real-valued continuously differentiable even functions on the closed interval [-1,1].

In fact heavy post-modification is a common way of avoiding ambiguity:

We consider the family of linear operators acting on the space of continuously differentiable functions defined on a bounded domain with a sufficiently smooth boundary and satisfying the homogeneous Dirichlet condition prescribed on its boundary.

Here a noun phrase headed by family contains 31 post-modifying words distributed across a prepositional phrase and an extensive participial construction.

In fact almost any page of ME will display noun phrases in nearly full structural patterns:

the unique solution of (1) that we found earlier (determiner + adjective + noun + prepositional phrase + relative clause)
a nonempty set of points residing on S (determiner + adjective + noun + prepositional phrase + participial phrase)
the best way to approach the problem (determiner + adjective + noun + infinitive phrase)

Grammar Working Together with Mathematical Notation

All of the basic sentence patterns of English readily accommodate mathematical symbols:

I.  The point x is in the interval $[a,b]$.
II.  The function f is continuous.
III.  The function f is a solution to (1).
IV.  The eigenvalues of f remained positive.
V.  The function f becomes a solution.
VI.  The point x lies in [a,b].
VII.  The solution crosses the x-axis.
VIII.  This form gives the solution f simplicity.
IX.  We call f smooth.
X.  We call this function f.

Symbols can serve as nouns functioning as the subjects or objects of verbs:

The product xy is increasing.
We seek the projection of P onto S.

Note how P and S operate syntactically as proper nouns taking no determiners.

Symbols can serve as verbs, here as the word divides:

Hence a ∣ b.

Other symbols that stand in for transitive verbs are ⇒ (implies), → (approaches), and ∊ (belongs to). Symbols can serve as entire clauses, here introduced by a colon:

So x is a positive number: x > 0.

A deeper observation is that English grammar carries part of the logical structure in a sentence such as

For every x ∊ X, there exists y ∊ Y such that P(x, y).

Indeed, reversing the order of the quantifiers changes the proposition, and this is reflected in the grammatical order.

Symbolic expression is particularly good at displaying calculations and stating exact relationships. Ordinary prose, on the other hand, helps one orient and motivate readers, bring out hidden structure, connect results to intuition, and communicate meaning in general.

Typography as a Linguistic Tool

In ME, typography carries semantic weight: formatting can establish hierarchy, and can signal the functions of certain words, phrases, or mathematical symbols.

One common convention is to set newly defined terms in boldface, although some authors prefer italics for this purpose. Italics also serve to emphasize logical relationships in commentary-style prose (e.g.,“The converse does not hold.”). Mathematical variables, particularly single-letter symbols such as a, are italicized to distinguish them from ordinary words. This convention helps prevent confusion between a mathematical variable and the determiner “a” (“let a be a real number”). Other typographic variations, such as small caps, can identify structural elements such as labels and headings. A smaller font may set off supplementary material. Calligraphic or script letters frequently denote sets, families, spaces, and operators.

Non-Arbitrariness of Mathematical Symbols

Although mathematical symbols may have been arbitrary in their origins, their use is subject to the pressures of convention. Some examples:

  • variables:  x, y, n, α
  • constants:  e, π, c
  • operations and operators:  +, −, ×, ÷, d/dx, ∫, ∑, ∇
  • relations:  =, ≠, <, ≤, ∼, ≅
  • logical notation:  ∧, ∨, ¬, ⇒, ∀, ∃
  • set-theoretic notation:  ∊, ⊆, ∪, ∩, ∅
  • functions:  f(x), g ∘ f, f−1
  • geometric notation:  ∠ABC, AB, ∥, ⊥
  • abstract structures:  (M, d), a metric space

Crucial in calculations are the grouping symbols ( ), [ ], and { }. Many textbooks prescribe the nesting order { [ ( ) ] }, but parentheses may be nested, as in ( ( ) ), without using brackets or braces. The broadly accepted principle is that nested expressions are evaluated from the inside out.

The symbol ∞ is interesting in the way it calls for explicit warnings about notation vs. ontology:

Note carefully that ∞ is not a number.

Typographical stacking, as seen in the symbols ± and ∓, sometimes compresses more than one algebraic alternative into a single orthographic unit. This is yet another way of turning spatial layout into syntax.

Use of Punctuation Marks

The punctuation marks of ordinary English prose sometimes acquire new meanings in ME. An example is the exclamation point (!) in its use to denote the factorial operation:

4! = 24

Ellipsis dots, commonly marking omitted words, hesitation, or unfinished thought in ordinary prose, often indicate continuation according to a pattern:

…, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, …
a1 + a2 + ··· + an

Note that the dots can be on the baseline or vertically centered. They can even be set vertically (⋮) or diagonally (⋱), as is often done with matrix notation. The colon can denote a ratio, define a set, or appear in function notation:

2:3
{x ∊ ℝ : x > 0}
f : [0, 1] → ℝ

We could say similar things about vertical bars, prime marks, asterisks, and so forth.

In ME, questions sometimes arise concerning punctuation at the boundaries between prose and symbolic notation. Whether a displayed equation ends with a terminal period, for example, is subject to editorial preference. (However — as one of the few normative comments I shall make here — my position is that punctuation around displayed mathematics should be treated as part of sentence structure rather than as an independent typographical matter.)

Quotation marks commonly delimit quoted material, but sometimes serve metalinguistically as scare quotes to express an author's distance from, or skepticism toward, some quoted expression.

The book offers a “proof” but skips a critical edge case.
Some texts treat this as an “exact” solution.
The author claims the final step is “obvious” but this reviewer must disagree.

Prominence of Subordination

While the role played by conditionals is enough to make this case, it is worth noting the other types of subordination that are common features of ME. These are arguably more central than their counterparts in ordinary scientific or academic English.

Relative clauses define and restrict mathematical objects:

Consider the subset of functions that satisfy (3).
Instead we use a space whose elements are equivalence classes of points.
We employ (2), which follows from Minkowski's inequality.

Complement clauses frequently express mathematical propositions:

We saw that S is precompact.
It follows that f is continuous.

In each case the that-clause supplies the proposition, while the main clause specifies its logical or argumentative status.

Adverbial clauses are quite common. Purpose clauses (introduced by that, so that, in order that, in order to, so as to, to + infinitive, or sometimes lest) subordinate mathematical operations to discourse objectives:

We showed those details back in §1 so that we might understand §2.
We show these details here in §3 so that we may understand §4.

Note how the modal distinction between may and might, characteristic of formal English, reflects the temporal relation between the two clauses.

Temporal and sequential subordinate clauses (introduced by before, after, now that, once, until, as soon as, as long as, when, whenever, as, since, or while) serve to organize inferences:

After substitution, we obtain …
Before using the transform, we verify that …
Now that we have established (1), it is natural to …

Concessive and contrastive subordination types (introduced by the conjunctions although, even if, even though, while, albeit, or notwithstanding that) are useful to articulate distinctions and prevent confusion:

While h is continuous, it is not differentiable.
Claim (2) is false although Claim (1) is true.

Concessive framing is often aimed at validating a reader's intuition before having to dismantle it from a rigorous standpoint:

Although it may seem that …

The quantitative nature of mathematics prompts extensive use of comparison and degree clauses. The former commonly occur with as and than, and can express equality as well as inequality. The correlative patterns as … as and so … as are useful for expressing positive and negative comparisons.

f(x) grows faster than g(x) [grows].
f(x) is as large as g(x) [is large].
f(x) is larger than g(x) [is large].
f(x) has the same properties as g(x) [has].
f(x) is not so large as g(x) [is large].

Degree clauses specify intensity or the extent to which a property holds, and typically have consequential interpretations:

B is so small that it can be neglected.

Similar constructions occur with such … that.

Of course there are frequent adverbial clauses of place, time, and circumstance:

When studying real functions, it is customary to treat continuity.
We have f(x) > 1 whenever x > 1.

Result clauses introduced by so that are obviously common:

Choose x so that f(x) = 0.
We have x > 2, so that f(x) > 3.

Manner is most often communicated by prepositional phrases, but sometimes a subordinate clause (introduced by as if or as though) is needed.

It seems as though we are done.
We shall proceed as if we were ignorant of the solution.

Overall, what distinguishes ME is not simply the frequency of subordinate clauses, but the extent to which subordination carries the very architecture of mathematical reasoning.

Concerning conditionals, we might note an interesting asymmetry between grammatical structure and logical structure. In “A if B,” the consequent is A, which is also the main clause; in “A only if B,” the consequent is B while A remains the main clause. So the grammatical main clause does not determine the logical consequent. This prevents us from assuming that the grammatical organization of an English sentence directly mirrors the logical organization of its corresponding conditional. We do not escape asymmetry by rewording as “A is necessary for B” and “A is sufficient for B.”

Discourse Structuring

ME has characteristic ways of constructing meaning and positioning the reader in relation to knowledge. Its main communicative purpose is to define objects, posit axioms, and then formulate and prove propositions. The standard of validity is logical deduction; the reader's main task is to absorb definitions, then to follow and verify reasoning. Interspersed with the more formal discourse structures can be informal discussions providing motivation, perspective, historical tidbits, etc. A distinctive contribution to cohesion and coherence is provided via the numbering of definitions, axioms, propositions (which may be variously called theorems, lemmas, or corollaries), remarks, worked examples, and equations.

The characteristic vocabulary of proof is of special interest here. ME has a rich inventory of expressions that signal specific inferential roles:

  • Introducing a premise:  suppose that, assume that, let, take, consider.
  • Introducing a construction:  define, set, choose, put, let, construct.
  • Drawing an inference:  hence, therefore, it follows that, thus, consequently, we conclude that.
  • Reversing direction:  conversely, on the other hand, to prove the converse.
  • Establishing equivalence:  if and only if, when and only when, these statements are equivalent.
  • Handling cases:  in the first case, otherwise, it remains to consider.
  • Contradiction:  suppose to the contrary that, this is impossible, a contradiction.
  • Closing an argument:  this proves the result, as required, hence the claim follows, the proof is complete.

Existential Constructions

ME regularly employs existential there plus a form of to be or exist and a noun phrase. These constructions bear close correspondence with the logical quantifier ∃.

There exists a solution with the desired property.

In the usual syntactic analysis, there is an expletive: it occupies the subject position without itself referring to an entity, while the noun phrase following the verb is the notional subject.

Inversion and Extraposition

Besides existential constructions, other methods are used to arrange information in a way that suits the logic of a mathematical statement. These include inversion

We seek a surface on which lie both P and Q.
Seldom do we solve for x explicitly.
On S lies an arbitrary point P.
Here is an example of a discontinuous function.
More convincing are the arguments in [56].

and extraposition

It is clear that x > 0.
It remains to prove that s = t.
It follows from (3) and (5) that (7) can be integrated to yield (8).

The Definite Article and Mathematical Identification

Unusual care is taken with the definite article the. A noun phrase with the normally presupposes that its referent has already been identified sufficiently. This, in turn, may require an existence and uniqueness argument.

There exists a solution of the equation.
The solution is positive.

In the first example, a solution indicates possible non-uniqueness. In the second example, uniqueness is implied (or tacitly claimed, or glossed over). So the definite article may mark the transition from mere existence to mathematical identification:

There exists a unique function f with the required property. The function f is continuous.

Of course, a referent can be made definite by previous discourse even when many objects of the same kind exist.

Let z = a + ib be a complex number. The number z is pure imaginary if a = 0.

Hence the in ME is best understood as marking identification rather than uniqueness per se.

Small Clauses and Mathematical Notation

ME makes frequent use of structures resembling small clauses: a subject and a predicate without an overt finite verb.

Assume x = 1.

Here x = 1 expresses a proposition but contains no conjugated verb. Compare with the more fully articulated construction

Assume that x is equal to 1.

Mathematical notation allows a proposition to be expressed compactly and to occupy a syntactic position that might otherwise be occupied by a finite clause.

Apposition and Mathematical Relations

ME makes extensive use of apposition, and symbolic relations can participate:

The relation F = ma is useful.
The condition x > 0 is necessary.
The fact that f is bounded is obvious.

Frequently a symbol will be placed in apposition to its English definition in order to bridge intuition and formalism:

Suppose we delete from the chosen interval I the resulting point x.

The Acquisition of Specialized or Narrowed Meanings

Some ordinary English words (group, field, open, mean, prime, ideal, immediate, compact, integral, complete, dense, almost everywhere, relation, normal, sense, rank, strictly, real, unity, …) acquire specialized meanings in ME.

Others (distinct, arbitrary, vanish, identically, behaves) retain something of their plain meanings while taking on narrower roles.

The word formal is an interesting case. To proceed in a purely formal way is to manipulate expressions based solely on their form. It is systematic, but the manipulations may not be justified. Examples are manipulating power series without considering convergence, somehow obtaining a possible solution to a differential equation without verifying it, differentiating under an integral sign, and integrating a series term by term. The interesting part is that a formal proof is a highly rigorous, logically structured argument! The resolution appears to be that in ME, formal refers to an adherence to rules, and in a purely formal development one follows a set of manipulative rules whether or not their application can be justified in the setting at hand.

The words weaker and stronger take on important specialized meanings when used in their logical senses. A statement S' is weaker than a statement S if S ⇒ S'. Likewise, S is stronger than S'. With reference to an implication

P ⇒ Q

then, we can say that “Q holds under weaker hypotheses” if there exists P' such that P ⇒ P' and P' ⇒ Q. In other words, the original conclusion Q holds even when we weaken the assumptions. We can say that “P implies a stronger conclusion” if there exists Q' such that P ⇒ Q' and Q' ⇒ Q. In this case P entails more information than Q.

Extraordinarily Precise Usage

In everyday English, even words like “any” can be ambiguous. ME treats non-mathematical terms with unusual precision. Ordinary meaning is retained, but logical contribution to a statement is expected to avoid the looseness or pragmatic flexibility that is possible in ordinary English.

The subordinating conjunction unless, for example, does not acquire a special mathematical meaning. But in ME it is understood in a logically precise way: P unless Q means not-Q implies P, or equivalently P or Q (in particular, not as Q implies not-P).

We can solve (1) unless f is discontinuous.

The statement means that if f is continuous then we can solve (1), not that if f is discontinuous then we cannot solve (1). Very common are statements of the form

When [condition]1, [consequence] unless [condition]2.
If [condition]1 then [consequence] unless [condition]2.

Logically these are (P ⇒ Q) ∨ R. The assertion is that if R is false then P ⇒ Q; nothing is asserted about P ⇒ Q if R is true.

When x = 0, x/y = 0 unless y = 0.

If y ≠ 0, then x = 0 implies x/y = 0. If, on the other hand, y = 0, then nothing is asserted about x/y when x = 0.

The phrase not unless means only if. Hence P not unless Q means P implies Q; it is not equivalent to the negation of P unless Q.

Another example. In mathematics one cannot afford to confuse contraries with contradictories. Two statements are contradictories when exactly one of them is true. For instance, x > 0 and x ≤ 0 are contradictories. Two statements are contraries when they cannot both be true, although they may both be false. For instance, x > 0 and x < 0 are contraries: both are false when x = 0. In ME, contraries are commonly described as mutually exclusive or incompatible. Contradictory statements, by contrast, are explicitly identified as such, or one is described as the negation of the other.

Mathematical Notation as a Syntactic Disambiguator

Consider the unfortunate sentence

Apply f and g to x.

This can be understood in several different ways: as forming the quantity f(g(x)), as forming g(f(x)), or as forming both f(x) and g(x). Mathematical notation provides compact ways of removing such ambiguity.

Grammatical Scope

ME must pay unusually close attention to grammatical scope: the portion of a sentence over which a word or phrase has semantic effect. A word may occur syntactically within one phrase while its interpretation extends over a larger constituent of the sentence. Negation provides an important example. Comparing the two statements

Not every prime number is odd.    [true; symbolic form ¬∀x(P(x) ⇒ O(x))]
Every prime number is not odd.    [false; symbolic form ∀x(P(x) ⇒ ¬O(x))]

we see that a relatively small change in the grammatical arrangement of words can change the logical structure of the whole proposition. ME is particularly sensitive to such distinctions because its sentences often combine quantifiers, negation, conditionals, and modal expressions whose scopes may interact.

Tense, Voice, and Person

ME strongly favors the present tense. The passive voice is not quite as central as in scientific English.

Because ME makes extensive use of the first-person plural pronoun we, it also makes efficient use of the supplementary -ing clause whose understood subject is the subject of the main clause:

Substituting into (1), we find the solution.
Noting that a ≠ 0, we complete the proof.

High Lexical Density

Lexical density measures the proportion of content words in a clause. A reader must recover relatively large amounts of information from relatively short passages of ME. For example, the sentence

The uniform convergence of the sequence guarantees the continuity of its limiting function.
packs seven lexical items (uniform, convergence, sequence, guarantees, continuity, limiting, function) into a single clause.

But, of course, even a short sentence like

This relation shows clearly that (6) converges if |x| < 1.

can be syntactically quite complex despite its low lexical density.

Formulaic Phrases

Here I mean phrases such as if and only if, and without loss of generality. The restrictive marker such that often serves bridge an existential claim to whatever specific conditions the object must satisfy. Other characteristic collocations include when and only when, suppose to the contrary that, for the sake of completeness, up to and including, follows at once, if it exists, one and only one, we cannot speak of, written more concisely as, can be expressed as, and completes the proof.

Without loss of generality (abbreviated WLOG), is a hard-hitting prepositional phrase in ME. When a mathematical statement or argument is symmetric with respect to two or more variables, one may often assume a particular ordering of those variables WLOG. For example, if m and n occupy interchangeable roles, we may take m ≤ n since the opposite ordering is covered by the same argument upon swapping the variables. This technique skirts unnecessary repetition while preserving the generality of the argument.

In a strange but understandable twist, “if” inside a definition actually means “if and only if” by convention.

Characteristic Use of the Imperative

ME often employs the imperative mood to guide the reader through a construction or argument: let x be a real number; choose an element of X; fix ε > 0; suppose that f is continuous; consider the following function. Seldom representing commands in the usual sense, such imperatives have their own characteristic discourse functions in ME:

Let introduces an object, assigns notation, or establishes a parameter.
Choose directs attention to an object selected from a specified collection.
Fix establishes an object to be held constant.
Suppose introduces a proposition for exploration or argument.
Assume establishes a deductive premise.
Consider directs attention to an object, construction, or case.
Take often functions as a less formal alternative to choose.

Note that imperative let licenses a bare infinitive complement: let x be a real number; let f denote the function; let P lie on S. The bare infinitive complement is often merely implied, as in let x ∊ S.

Extended Use of where

In its most familiar use, the relative adverb where has a literal spatial meaning:

Let S be the region where f is defined.

But ME also uses where to introduce a specifying clause, even when the antecedent is not a place:

Let x = 2a, where a is the radius of the cylinder.

This allows a short relative clause to supplement a mathematical statement without interrupting syntactic flow.

Syntactic Variety in Definitions

ME permits considerable syntactic variation in formal definitions, allowing writers to avoid monotony.

By definition, f is smooth if …
We call f smooth if …
By a smooth function f we mean …
We say that f is smooth if …
A function f is said to be smooth if …
A function f is called smooth if …
Definition: f is smooth if …

These options differ in surface grammar, showing how a concept may be introduced by an adverbial phrase (by definition), by an active verb with a direct object (we call), by a prepositional phrase (by a smooth function), by a that-clause (we say that), by a passive construction (is said to be), or by a verbless label (Definition:).

Note that mathematical notation can remain fixed while surrounding English grammar varies substantially. A mathematical proposition is often invariant under syntactic transformation, while the choice of construction affects rhythm and emphasis.

In ME, the verb to be is frequently elaborated into what we might call “definitional copulas.” Examples are is denoted by, is represented by, is known as, is called, is written as, is termed, and is referred to as.

Implicit Universal Quantification

Unlike pure logic, ME often leaves universal quantification implicit:

x > y and y > z imply x > z

Unbound variables in a definition are universally quantified by default.

Sensitivity to Choice of Preposition

It is not surprising that prepositions play central roles in ME, as they often convey precise logical, structural, or functional relationships.

  • location:  in the disk, at the endpoints
  • proximity:  near the corner
  • direction:  converges to zero, moves along the x-axis
  • time sequence:  during the iterative process, before we begin
  • association & exclusion:  a number of properties, without repetition, except where
  • spatial relationship:  between the curve and the axis, below the plane
  • scope & quantification:  for all real numbers, over the entire interval
  • condition:  under the assumption, necessary conditions on f
  • purpose:  for the sake of convenience, with a view toward efficiency
  • agency & means:  by virtue of Theorem 1, by means of the Fourier transform
  • operation:  multiply by a weighting factor
  • dependence:  depends on, varies with
  • relation:  with respect to, relative to
  • derivation & source:  by Theorem 2, from the definition
  • subdivision:  divide the development into steps
  • opposition:  with or against the flow
  • causality:  determined by, follows from

Spatial Metaphor

ME relies heavily on spatial language to convey abstract concepts: a function approaches a limit, a sequence converges to a point, a set contains an element, one space embeds into another, an argument proceeds by induction, a result follows from a hypothesis, a proof breaks down in a particular case, a function maps one set to another, a sequence diverges to infinity, a curve lies on a surface, a property holds across a domain, a value falls within an interval, a proof bridges the gap between two lemmas, two structures collapse into one, a solution branches, a parameter ranges over an interval, an argument reaches a contradiction, a theorem extends an earlier result. Many such expressions are now so conventional that their metaphorical origins are barely noticeable.

Conjunctive Adverbs as Mere Conclusion Indicators

Words like consequently, accordingly, whence, whereupon, thence, and thus can carry slight nuances in careful academic prose. In ME however, despite its formality, these are often lumped together with therefore as logical conclusion indicators and mixed for purposes of style.

Careful Counterfactuals and Suppositions

Conditionals as plain facts can be stated in the indicative mood.

If p=1, then q = 1/2.

Clearly there is uncertainty as to the fact of whether the condition holds here, but if it does then the consequence is entailed. The statement does not go out of its way to introduce a sense of unreality or doubt, or depict a situation where we are merely imagining something. This is also the case with the following conditional, even though past time is referred to:

If the reader did this, he or she committed an error.

But other types of hypothetical bring unreality to the fore as a way of proceeding with an argument.

When reference is made to present time, the patterns are

If a condition were to hold or could hold, (then) something should or would follow.
Something should or would follow if a condition were to hold.
Were a condition to hold, (then) something would or should follow.
Something would or should follow were a condition to hold.

In this way the past tense is used as the marker of unreality in English:

The case would be altered at once if the ring were not quite uniform, e.g. if it were slightly thicker at one point.

Sir Horace Lamb, The Dynamical Theory of Sound

Again this was for reference to present time. For reference to past time, the past perfect is used; the paradigm is something like

If a condition had held, (then) something should/would have followed.
Something should/would have followed if a condition had held.
Had a condition held, (then) something should/would have followed.
Something should/would have followed had a condition held.

Note the “had / have” pairing.

Of course we never really know what the future holds, but at times we may want to express a supposition and state its consequence as a mere mental construct, or as something doubtful. In this case we use the subjunctive in both clauses of the conditional:

If it occurred / were to occur / should occur, (then) we should / it would ….
If we were to do this, we would find ….

Although the subjunctive mood is often ignored, its use can help writers keep their thinking straight. The same is true for choice of imperative verb. There is no mental conflict in saying “assume x is 1” if unity is a permissible value of x. But “assume x is infinite” can be problematic if the statement is clearly impossible or we intend to demonstrate its falsehood. In contrast, “suppose x were infinite” frames an imaginative exploration.

For suppose x were infinite. Then … But …, which would be absurd. Consequently … □

The question is whether we can best keep our thoughts straight by “assuming” that something “is” while setting out to show that it actually “isn’t”.

The Present Subjunctive

Beyond the contrast between plain facts and total unreality, ME occasionally employs the present subjunctive to signal a state of “mere possibility” — something imagined or conceived to enable a further statement, without implying that the condition is impossible or unlikely. For verbs other than be, the third-person singular present subjunctive is distinguished from the indicative by the absence of the -s ending. To illustrate the precision this affords, consider the verb lie (past tense lay):

For if P lies on S — present indicative: in some cases P does indeed lie on S.

For if P lie on S — present subjunctive: P is imagined on S; it may or may not be so in reality.

For if P lay on S — past subjunctive: in reality P cannot reside on S.

This classical usage allows precise calibration of the level of doubt or imagination. The present subjunctive also appears in adverbial purpose clauses (e.g., “it is necessary to do so, that the effort might be successful”) and in frozen constructs such as “be that as it may.”

While the present subjunctive has become less common in contemporary writing, its presence in ME serves to distinguish between implications based on possible conditions and those based on factual conditions.

Semantic Patterns

The semantics of ME characteristically rely on explicitly stipulated meanings and carefully specified relationships among abstract objects. Definitions are especially prominent. They often specify the conditions under which an object belongs to a class:

We write S = { x : P(x) }.

Domains are often made explicit rather than left to contextual inference:

∀ y ∊ S,  P(y).

But some expressions remain context-dependent:

We assume all functions are sufficiently smooth.

The imperative “let … be” typically introduces an object and assigns it a discourse referent:

Let P be a point on S. We call P a critical point if …

In comparison with Scientific English, there is a notable absence of modals qualifying predicates. Compare

If the temperature increases, so may the reaction rate.
If x ∊ ℝ, then x2 ≥ 0.

Epistemic Humility

Hedging is not as common in ME as in Scientific English, but it can occur in exploratory writing:

It appears that …
This is consistent with …
The conjecture seems to hold for …
There is no clear evidence of …
The plot suggests that …

Other examples of epistemic humility:

To the best of our knowledge …
We have not established whether …
This suggests, but does not prove, that …
We may be overlooking a simpler approach to …

The point of such phrasing is to accurately represent the epistemic status of each claim.

Conventional Language of Examples and Counterexamples

In mathematics there is a stark logical asymmetry between illustration and refutation. An example can illuminate a general claim but cannot prove it; a counterexample, on the other hand, dismantles a claim entirely. ME uses brief but characteristic phrasings to frame the role of a coming example:

For example, …
For instance, …
As an illustration, …
A counterexample is provided by …

The Language of Case Analysis

A characteristic of ME is the explicit announcement and orderly enumeration of cases, rather than a silent movement from one possibility to another.

There are three cases to consider.
The proof must treat several cases.

Often the cases are signposted one by one:

In the first place, …
Finally, …

But they may be introduced individually through separate conditional clauses:

There are three cases. If α < π, then …; if α = π, then …; finally, if α > π then ….

Here the repeated if clauses separate the three branches of the argument.

In more complex arguments, there may be levels of case analysis with subcases nested inside cases.

Especially important in careful ME is the exhastive treatment of edge cases. When an audience cannot be left to infer what happens in exceptional, limiting, or degenerate situations, an explicit treatment is given. Qualifications, parenthetical remarks, and carefully worded provisos ensure that the mathematical statement remains valid in every relevant circumstance.

… with the understanding that …
If c is an endpoint, …
… except for the trivial case when S is empty.
… whenever the right-hand side is finite.
For all but finitely many points of I, we have …

The type of modified universal quantification seen in the last example is particularly useful.

Proof Conclusion Markers

A proof may end with a statement of the result, a typographical marker (commonly the empty square □ or the black square ■), or an explicit sign-off:

… as desired.
… as required.
… as was to be proved.
… and the proof is complete.
… and the assertion follows.
… and the theorem is established.
… which completes the proof.
… a contradiction.

These are all formulaic equivalents of Q.E.D. (Quod erat demonstrandum).

Parallel Compression with Parentheticals or “Respectively”

Concise ME can express parallel relationships in a single sentence, with the word “respectively” used as a device to pair items with their corresponding counterparts in the order in which they appear.

A number x is called positive or negative if x > 0 or x < 0, respectively.

This not only avoids repetition, but puts statements into the same syntactic form so that the reader can compare them directly. Small differences in notation, such as > vs. < in the preceding example, receive emphasis when the surrounding grammatical structure is invariant. Instead of using “respectively,” an author may put alternatives in parentheses:

The function g is strictly increasing (decreasing) if g(x) < g(y) (g(x) > g(y)) whenever x < y.

Mathematical Rhetoric

A mathematical argument is both a logical structure and an object of communication. A proof may be valid but poorly presented, elegant but incomplete, or persuasive but wrong. Hence mathematical reasoning, mathematical exposition, and mathematical persuasion intersect near something we could call mathematical rhetoric. Of these practices, mathematical persuasion may raise the most eyebrows. Yet responsible persuasion is plainly important in ME. Anyone who views mathematical discourse from a humanistic perspective can see the roles of logos (reasoned argument), ethos (intellectual credibility), and pathos (intellectual engagement).

For example, ethos can be built through signs of reliability or expertise. An author might begin by connecting the work to an established intellectual tradition, or mention respected figures such as Gauss or Euler. Even using standard notation can signal “professional language of the field.” Pathos, though harder to spot in ME, can come from shaping the ideas into an engaging story with a sense of tension. A problem might be presented as an old mystery or a vexing challenge; subsequent revelation of an “elegant” approach, bringing both relief and a sense of beauty, then turns a dry logical exercise into an emotional experience of discovery.

Morphology of Mathematical Terminology

The specialized vocabulary of mathematics is often formed using prefixes, roots, and suffixes. For example, isomorphism combines Greek elements iso- (“same”) and morph- (“form” or “structure”), along with the noun-forming suffix -ism, while isomorphic uses the adjective-forming suffix -ic.

Structural Cross-Referencing

Frequent use of chapter and section citations (cf. Ch 2, § 114) can anchor text within a tightly interconnected system. These are often embedded directly into sentences as parentheticals to avoid breaking narrative flow. References can be forward or backward relative to the current text position. Page or proposition or equation numbers can be used as well, producing a dense, hierarchical numbering system (Ch 2, § 13, part (ii) of Theorem I). The double section sign (§§) is standard mathematical shorthand for plural sections (§§ 11–14).

Uses of Nominalization

M.A.K. Halliday pointed out that science develops partly through process-to-thing conversion. Nominalization is a creation of new linguistic objects through which phenomena become available for thought. In other words, language is not merely a medium for communicating existing scientific knowledge gained through direct experience with nature; it also plays an active role in the formation of that knowledge. The transformation of the verb react into the noun reaction is not merely a grammatical change. It also involves processes of reification and abstraction. Reactions become thing-like entities that can have rates, energy changes, pathways, reversibilities or irreversibilities, applications, and so on. In this sense, one might say that science itself is partly a linguistic object.

ME exhibits distinctive tendencies in its employment of this resource, reflecting its emphasis on abstraction, logical relation, and mathematical objects. By nominalizing a verbal phrase — for example changing “we transform the equation” into “the transformation of the equation” — the resulting expression can be treated as a mathematical object in its own right (e.g., “the transformation preserves handedness”). Acts of rearranging infinite series can be studied as rearrangements. Similarly, ME frequently converts properties into abstract nouns, as seen in the nominalization of adjectives such as regular, bounded, and symmetric into regularity, boundedness, and symmetry. This process allows a property to be isolated, named, and reasoned about independently of any particular object possessing it. Consequently, nominalization in ME is deeply intertwined with the conceptual structure of mathematics itself.

Subregisters

A number of distinct subregisters of ME are discernible, with variations among research articles, monographs, technical reports, textbooks, lecture notes, popular expositions, and informal correspondence.

As an example of a historical and local subregister, we could take ME from the British Victorian era.

Through the centre of a sphere and any two points on the surface a plane can be drawn; and only one plane can be drawn, except when the two points are the extremeties of a diameter of the sphere, and then an infinite number of such planes can be drawn.

I. Todhunter, Spherical Trigonometry
Perusing the work of authors such as Todhunter, Horace Lamb, George Chrystal, Edward Routh, and George Salmon, one encounters many distinctive stylistic aspects. Long periodic sentences abound, often comprising several subordinate and relative clauses before the main assertion is reached. Participial constructions, particularly the nominative absolute, are frequently employed to introduce mathematical conditions or dependencies without interrupting the flow of the argument:

… d being the shortest distance from P to L.

Elliptical participial constructions, such as Lamb's “supposed drawn vertically upwards,” show a marked preference for compression over the more explicit relative clauses of contemporary ME. The vocabulary incorporates archaic intransitive verbs such as obtain and prevail, formal connectives such as in virtue of, in consequence of, and whence, and expressions such as put in evidence that betray the influence of French analytical mechanics. Extensive use is made of formal pronominal references, including the latter and the point in question, as well as interruptive prepositional phrases. We may see shew instead of show, suffixes instead of subscripts, formulae instead of formulas, analysis situs instead of topology, and “&c., &c.” instead of and so on. Connectives such as Again, For, and Whence may seem odd to the contemporary reader. Litotes such as not inconsiderable difficulties lend the exposition a characteristic air of understatement. Latin-inspired bans on split infinitives and sentence-ending prepositions are respected. We could continue this list for a long time.

M.A.K. Halliday observed that new language registers arise to meet emerging social needs. With this in mind it would not be correct to speak of an AI-Slop Subregister of ME, but I do expect an ever increasing intrusion of the existing register by machine synthesized text. What qualifies as AI slop in mathematical writing? I would say that its main feature is a characteristic combination of rhetorical confidence and defective rigor. I'm immediately suspicious when I encounter empty declarative grandeur or what one might term the tapestry and symphony lexicon: “deep underlying structure,” “far-reaching and profound,” “elegant framework,” “powerful perspective,” “intricate tapestry,” “nuanced interplay,” “rich landscape,” “beautiful and illuminating connection,” “unifying perspective,” “subtle dance,” “hidden harmony,” “unifying perspective,” “delicate architecture,” “vast web,” …. Ugh.

An interesting question is whether a mathematics book translated from, say, Russian into English is an example of ME. While the English of the translation may be impeccable, a linguistic register is not defined merely grammar; the resulting book may still embody Russian preferences concerning mathematical exposition or pedagogy. In some ways the translation may retain the source material's rhetorical architecture even after every sentence has been rendered into idiomatic English. In such a case maybe the safe thing to call it would be “an example of Russian mathematical discourse expressed in English.”

Pedagogical Features

Textbook writing has its own characteristic features.

Collaborative tone is built via the first-person plural. It's interesting to note how many functions this performs:

  • Observation and recognition: “We see that,” “We observe that,” “We have,” “We now have.”
  • Deduction and inference: “We infer that,” “We obtain,” “We get,” “We deduce,” “We conclude.”
  • Statement of purpose: “We wish to show that,” “We must first show,” “We shall prove,” “We want to establish.”
  • Introduction of procedures and cases: “We start by defining,” “We consider the case a > 0,” “We proceed by induction.”
  • Organization and transition: “We now proceed,” “We shall restrict ourselves to,” “We are now ready to state a generalization.”
  • Presentation of results: “With the aid of Lemma 1 we can now establish,” “We now state the following theorem,” “Hence we conclude that.”

Of course there is much pedagogical metadiscourse (“signposting”) to help the student navigate:

In other words, …
That is, …
In detail, …
The final section may be omitted on a first reading.

In fact, unlike standard prose, mathematical prose constantly announces what it is about to do before doing it:

The present chapter is devoted to …
We now show that …
We seek to prove …
There are two parts to the proof.
A few words regarding notation are in order.

Collocations such as we shall not attempt to and we restrict ourselves to explicitly bound the discourse.

We see Socratic questioning, sometimes in short parenthetical nudges:

Should we just assume existence?
Under what circumstances could this be problematic?
(how does this satisfy (12)?)
(why?)

Such conversational technique can expose hidden assumptions, guide inquiry, and serve as motivation.

Full rigor or completeness may be sacrificed here and there for pedagogical simplification:

For definiteness and simplicity we prove this in two dimensions.
(and will not be given here)
(check the details)

The occasional parenthetical gloss provides readers with an accessible interpretation of unfamiliar or abstract language. We may encounter value-laden adjectives such as important, irrelevant, untrustworthy, excellent, inappropriate, embarrassing, naive, or powerful. Phrases like as expected and as might be anticipated set epistemic benchmarks and serve as subtle nudges to build mathematical maturity. Explicitly labeled hints offer strategic starting points for problem solving. Skip paths may be outlined for readers having limited goals (“Readers interested in applications can safely omit Chaper 4.”), and compassionate directives may bound the student's task:

Do not try to compute λ.
It is not required to show that …

We sometimes even see historical motivation.

Contractions

Despite the insistence of traditional style guides, contractions are often used in ME to avoid stilted prose, particularly in negative questions:

Haven't we missed something? Shouldn't there be multiple roots?

Non-English Expressions

Non-English expressions appear frequently in ME; they include a fortiori, a priori, a posteriori, ad hoc, ad infinitum, ceteris paribus, non sequitur, per se, petitio principii, and reductio ad absurdum, along with the usual abbreviations like cf., and etc.

Mathematical English as an Integrated System

The foregoing features of ME are not independent and chosen by convention. They are interrelated aspects of a register aimed at a particular type of activity. I believe that three aspects of its task deserve emphasis.

First, ME establishes and expresses logical dependencies. The word order in a phrase like

for every x there exists y such that
is not mere stylistic preference; it reflects the essential fact that y may depend on x.

Second, ME enables mathematical actions. Imperative-based expressions (especially the canonical sipulative Let …) establish objects, parameters, and premises from which reasoning can start. Conjunctive adverbs and other signposts mark inferential relationships and organize arguments. These and other grammatical elements are not simply descriptors of mathematical activity; they are direct participants.

Third, ME coordinates natural language with mathematical notation. Meaning is distributed across linguistic and notational elements — from symbols and words, through phrases, clauses, and sentences, to paragraphs and other discourse structures.

These three aspects are closely connected. Again, ME is not just English plus mathematical terminology and symbols. It is a specialized register adapted to formulating, developing, and communicating mathematical thought.

Summary

ME is a specialized register for mathematical exposition. It uses precise terminology, explicit definitions and hypotheses, and conventional notation to make claims and their logical relationships clear. Its characteristic style combines structured prose with mathematical symbols, integrating formulas into sentences without sacrificing readability. Exposition typically proceeds through a sequence of definitions, assumptions, arguments, and conclusions, with logical connectors and signposting to guide the reader. Proof-writing conventions and cross-references help establish continuity, while concise, disciplined language keeps attention on the mathematical content. The register varies with its audience and purpose: it may be formal and detached in a proof, or more conversational and example-driven in teaching. In either case, its goal is to connect intuition with abstraction and to make the reasoning understandable and verifiable.
